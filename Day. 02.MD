DAY 2: RISK MANAGEMENT

1. Definition of Risk

Risk is the potential for loss, damage, or harm to an organization, system, network, or information.

In cybersecurity, risk occurs when a threat exploits a vulnerability and causes negative consequences.

Risk Formula:

Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact

Risk can also be expressed as:

Risk = Likelihood × Impact

- Likelihood: The probability that an event or threat will occur.
- Impact: The consequences or damage that may result from the event.

2. Key Components of Risk

A. Threats

Threats are anything that could potentially cause harm to a system, network, or organization.

Examples:
- Malware
- Hackers
- Insider threats

B. Vulnerabilities

Vulnerabilities are weaknesses in a system, network, or application that can be exploited by attackers.

Examples:
- Outdated software
- Weak passwords
- Unpatched systems

C. Impact

Impact refers to the consequences or damage that may occur when a risk materializes.

Examples:
- Financial loss
- Data theft
- Reputational damage
- System downtime

D. Likelihood

Likelihood refers to the probability or chance that a threat or harmful event will actually occur.

Example: A company using weak passwords may have a higher likelihood of experiencing unauthorized access.

3. Risk Assessment

Risk assessment is the systematic process of identifying, analyzing, and evaluating risks to determine their severity and priority.

Steps in Risk Assessment:

1. Identifying: Identify the things that need to be protected (your assets).
2. Vulnerabilities: Find weaknesses in systems, networks, applications, or security processes.
3. Understand Potential Consequences: Conduct an impact analysis to understand what could happen if a risk occurs.
4. Evaluate and Prioritize Risks: Determine the likelihood and impact of risks so that the most serious risks can be addressed first.

4. Risk Management Strategies

Risk management involves identifying risks and deciding how to handle them.

There are four common risk management strategies:

A. Avoidance

Avoidance means eliminating an activity or situation that creates a particular risk.

Example: A company stops using an insecure application that exposes sensitive information.

B. Mitigation

Mitigation means reducing the likelihood or impact of a risk.

Example: Installing security updates, using strong passwords, and enabling multi-factor authentication.

C. Transfer

Transfer means shifting some of the financial consequences of a risk to another party.

Examples:
- Insurance
- Outsourcing certain services

D. Acceptance

Acceptance means acknowledging a risk and choosing to live with it when the organization considers it manageable.

Example: A company accepts a low-impact risk because the cost of fixing it is greater than the potential damage.

5. Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence is information that helps organizations predict, prevent, identify, and respond to cyber threats.

Why Threat Intelligence Matters:

- Helps organizations stay ahead of attackers.
- Helps identify potential threats.
- Improves incident response.
- Supports proactive cybersecurity defense.
- Helps organizations make better risk management decisions.

6. Types of Threat Intelligence

A. Strategic Threat Intelligence

Strategic threat intelligence focuses on long-term cyber threats, trends, risks, and the overall cybersecurity landscape.

Who uses it?
- CEO
- Senior Management
- Board of Directors

Example: A CEO uses threat intelligence to make decisions about cybersecurity budgets, policies, and hiring security professionals.

B. Tactical Threat Intelligence

Tactical threat intelligence focuses on the tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) used by attackers.

Who uses it?
- Security Team
- Security Managers
- Security Engineers

Example: A security team uses threat intelligence to improve firewall rules and strengthen defenses against attackers.

C. Operational Threat Intelligence

Operational threat intelligence focuses on specific threats, attack campaigns, threat actors, or planned attacks.

Who uses it?
- SOC Analysts
- Threat Analysts
- Incident Responders

Example: A SOC Analyst investigates a phishing campaign and identifies malicious emails and links.

7. Sources of Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence can be gathered from different sources:

A. Open Source Intelligence (OSINT)

Information gathered from publicly available sources.

B. Threat Feeds

Regularly updated information about known threats, malicious IP addresses, domains, malware, and other indicators of compromise.

C. Dark Web

Information gathered from dark web activities and communities that may reveal cyber threats, stolen data, or criminal activities.

D. Sharing Communities

Groups or organizations that share cybersecurity information, threat indicators, and intelligence to help members protect themselves.

8. Using Threat Intelligence

A. Proactive Defense

Threat intelligence can be used to prevent attacks before they happen.

Example: Blocking known malicious IP addresses or domains.

B. Incident Response

Threat intelligence helps security teams detect threats faster, investigate incidents, respond effectively, and recover from attacks.

C. Improving Risk Management

Threat intelligence helps organizations understand current threats and make better decisions about identifying, assessing, and managing cybersecurity risks.

CONCLUSION

Risk management helps organizations identify, assess, prioritize, and manage cybersecurity risks.

Threat intelligence provides useful information about cyber threats, helping organizations improve their defenses, respond to attacks, and make better security decisions.
